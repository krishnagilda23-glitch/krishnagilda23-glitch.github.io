Design + Organisational Thinking

Design for Independence

What if the best measure of our work is how well it continues without us?

By Krishna Gilda•25 April, 2023•6 min read

Have you ever been contacted by a client about a minor detail they should have been able to handle themselves? Or felt that your work was only as reliable as your presence?

I used to think that indispensability was a sign of value. Then a piece of career advice from my father gave me a question I could not ignore.

The advice—and the problem inside it

“Work to make your employer dependent on you.”

I took those words to heart. Over time, however, I began to see the drawbacks of creating dependency. If a process succeeds only because one person is present, it is fragile by design. It restricts the team, complicates transitions and eventually restricts the person everyone depends on.

That tension led me to the idea of Design for Independence.

The paradox

Making yourself replaceable can make your contribution more valuable.

What is Design for Independence?

In an employee–employer relationship, it means creating processes and systems that remain useful without our constant involvement. The system should carry the knowledge, intent and safeguards that would otherwise exist only in one person’s head.

This takes planning, iteration and a long-term view. It also forces us to make decisions transparent, introduce checks and balances, and clarify what should happen when circumstances change.

  1. 01

    Make the process visible

    Document the reasoning, sequence and ownership behind the work so that knowledge is shared instead of remaining implicit.

  2. 02

    Preserve the intent

    Explain which qualities must remain consistent and why, not only which steps someone should repeat.

  3. 03

    Create safe boundaries

    Give people enough control to act independently while protecting the decisions that should not change.

  4. 04

    Plan for the handover

    Treat transitions as a design condition from the beginning rather than an emergency at the end.

Independence creates resilience

Transparent systems make transitions less disruptive when employees change roles or leave. They also create space for growth: people can move towards larger responsibilities instead of remaining trapped as the only person capable of completing a recurring task.

Designing this way compels us to contribute more than the immediate deliverable. We have to anticipate possible failures, make our reasoning understandable and leave behind a structure that others can improve.

The work is not truly complete when the task is finished. It is complete when the value can continue.

Independence is not the same as automation

There is a risk in pushing the idea too far. A highly mechanised process can remove judgment, creativity and the human knowledge that helps people respond to unfamiliar situations.

The goal is therefore not to eliminate human input. It is to separate routine dependence from meaningful participation.

01

Systemise

  • Repeated instructions and predictable decisions
  • File structures, standards and approval checks
  • Knowledge needed for routine maintenance
02

Protect

  • Human judgment in unfamiliar circumstances
  • Space for interpretation, creativity and dissent
  • Conversation around consequential decisions

The principle extends beyond employment

Design for Independence applies wherever intent must survive beyond its original creator.

  1. 01

    Brand identity

    Brand guidelines preserve a recognisable essence even after the original designers move on.

  2. 02

    Repairable products

    Platforms such as iFixit help people maintain products without permanent dependence on repair services or the brand itself.

  3. 03

    Durable organisations

    Apple, Microsoft, Google and Braun continued to evolve after their founders stepped away because their values were carried by institutions, cultures and systems.

A CAD thought experiment

Industrial designers encounter small dependencies throughout production. A manufacturer may need to change a fillet radius or adjust another minor CAD detail, yet still depend on the designer to reopen the model and make the modification.

What if a limited-functionality CAD tool allowed the manufacturer to make those changes independently—within boundaries defined by the designer?

01

Set limits

The designer identifies which dimensions and features may be changed safely.

02

Share control

The manufacturer receives only the adjustments needed for production.

03

Validate

The software checks every change against the original constraints.

04

Keep moving

Minor corrections continue without interrupting the designer’s larger work.

The manufacturer gains useful autonomy, the designer retains control over intent, and both spend less time coordinating minor revisions. It is one small example of how independence can be designed into a tool.

Why the idea matters now

AI is freeing more processes from direct human dependence. That makes the question more important, not less. We need to decide which knowledge should be embedded in tools, which decisions require people, and how responsibility moves between them.

The opportunity is to create systems that are self-sufficient without becoming thoughtless: resilient enough to continue, transparent enough to question and flexible enough to improve.

Conclusion

Design the value to outlast your presence

Design for Independence asks us to leave behind more than a finished task. It asks us to leave clarity, usable knowledge and room for the next person to act.

Done carefully, it can produce processes, products and organisations that are more resilient, more sustainable and more capable of growth—without removing the human judgment that made them valuable in the first place.

The aim is not to make people unnecessary. It is to make progress possible without unnecessary dependence.
